# What Is “Food Noise,” and Why Do People Talk About It?

Planning dinner is ordinary. So is looking forward to a favorite meal. But some people describe thoughts about food that keep interrupting the rest of the day: what to eat next, whether to eat, how to resist eating, or what they wish they had not eaten.

“Food noise” has become a popular name for that experience. It can give people language for something they struggled to explain. It can also become confusing when every craving or hungry afternoon gets placed under the same label.

Researchers are still working out how to define and measure it.

## A useful description, with boundaries still being studied

A 2025 expert paper proposed describing food noise in terms of unwanted, persistent food-related thoughts that interfere with everyday life. The work, funded by the telehealth company Ro, also explored ways to measure the experience. [The food-noise definition paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41387-025-00382-x).

A later theoretical review emphasized how much remains uncertain, including the causes of food noise and how treatments might change it. The review considered several possible explanations rather than identifying a single established mechanism. [Food noise: a theoretical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42772688/).

It is therefore better understood as an emerging research concept and a description of experience than as a diagnosis you can confirm from a social-media quiz.

## Hunger, enjoyment, and distress are different

You can be hungry without spending hours preoccupied with food. You can enjoy cooking, plan a celebration meal, or notice a bakery without that experience being a problem.

The useful questions concern how intrusive the thoughts feel and what they interfere with. Can you return your attention to work or a conversation? Do food decisions take up time you wish you could spend elsewhere? Does the experience feel distressing?

These questions can help describe the issue to a professional. They are not a diagnostic test or a score that determines whether you should receive medication.

It is also worth describing the circumstances. Thoughts that become intense after a day of missed meals are a different starting point for a conversation from thoughts that feel relentless even when you are eating regularly. The label alone does not explain either experience.

## Why GLP-1 medicines entered the conversation

People taking GLP-1 medicines have reported changes in hunger, cravings, and the mental attention given to food. Researchers are studying those reports, but different studies measure different things.

In the 2026 INFORM survey, 550 US adults taking injectable semaglutide recalled their food-related thoughts before treatment and reported their current experience. Responses suggested less food noise after starting treatment. Because the comparison relied on recollection and had no randomized control group, it cannot prove that medication caused all of the reported change. Novo Nordisk employees were among the authors, and the company paid for the analysis. [INFORM survey](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42217114/).

That distinction does not invalidate someone’s relief. It matters when moving from “this is what I experienced” to “this will happen for everyone.”

The presence or absence of that feeling is also not a stand-alone way to judge whether a treatment is appropriate or working. Medication decisions need the broader health picture.

## Food noise is not another name for binge eating disorder

Binge eating disorder involves recurring episodes of eating unusually large amounts with a sense of losing control. Food-related preoccupation and binge eating can be part of the same person’s experience, but the terms do not mean the same thing.

The [NIDDK’s explanation of binge eating disorder](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/binge-eating-disorder/definition-facts) describes it as a condition that warrants assessment. It can affect people across body sizes, and most people with obesity do not have it.

If your concerns include loss-of-control eating, purging, severe restriction, or substantial distress, describe those directly to a healthcare professional. Calling everything food noise can make it harder to identify the help you actually need.

## You do not need the perfect term to ask for help

A brief account of an ordinary day may be more useful than adopting a label. Explain when the thoughts appear, how they feel, whether you have been able to eat regularly, and what they prevent you from doing.

You might say, “I spend much of the afternoon thinking about what I will eat, and it is hard to focus,” or “I am constantly negotiating with myself about food.” These are examples of ways to describe an experience, not patient quotations.

The aim is to make the problem understandable. You do not have to prove that your thoughts meet an internet definition before they deserve attention.
